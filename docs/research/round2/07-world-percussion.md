## 2. Extracted terminology (round B)

### 2.1 Afro-Cuban — conga / tumbadora

**2.1.1 Drum names by size and role** — source C2 (Wikipedia *Conga*, size table) and C3.

| Name | Head diameter | Role |
|---|---|---|
| supertumba / rebajador | 14 in (36 cm) | largest |
| tumba / salidor | 12–12.5 in (30–32 cm) | lowest pitched; in rumba the marking drum |
| conga / tres dos | 11.5–12 in (29–30 cm) | middle |
| quinto | ~11 in (28 cm) | lead, highest; "constantly punctuating, coloring, and accenting" (C2) |
| requinto | <10 in (25 cm) | smaller lead |
| requinto chico | 9 in (23 cm) | smallest; "played while hanging from a shoulder strap" (C2) |

C2 also records the *son cubano* / charanga naming, which is **not** by size but by the bongo
convention: `macho` (higher) and `hembra` (lower), optionally `tercera`. In rumba,
C3 gives the three roles as `quinto` (lead, highest), `tres dos` (middle),
`tumba`/`salidor` (lowest).

**2.1.2 Stroke set — the Spanish nomenclature** — source C2, verbatim list.

| Spanish | English | Physical description (verbatim where quoted) |
|---|---|---|
| tono abierto | open tone | "played with the four fingers near the rim of the head, producing a clear resonant sound with a higher pitch than muffled and bass tones" |
| tono ahogado / apagado | muffled tone | "like the open tone, it is made by striking the drum with the four fingers, but holding the fingers against the head to muffle the tone" |
| tono seco abierto | open slap | slap variant, fingers rebound |
| tono seco cerrado (*tapao*, *galleta*, *quemao*) | closed slap | slap variant, fingers stay |
| tono seco presionado | pressed slap | slap with pressure |
| tono bajo de palma | bass tone | "played with the full palm, in a slightly cupped position, somewhat off center on the head. It produces a low muted sound" |
| tono de talón de mano | heel tone | heel of the palm |
| toque de dedos | fingers tone | fingers |
| toque de punta de dedos | fingertips tone | fingertips |
| tono de uñas | fingernails tone | "played with the tip of the nails, usually finger by finger in quick succession, starting with the pinky" |
| tono deslizado | glissando / "moose call" | "done by rubbing the third finger, supported by the thumb, across the head of the drum. The finger is sometimes moistened with saliva or sweat" |

`manoteo` = the heel–toe rocking alternation of palm-heel and fingertips producing a roll
(round A Spanish-language sources give it as "movimiento de balancín entre el talón de la
mano y la punta de los dedos"). The muted slap is produced by "playing an open tone while
the other hand rests on the drumhead, which produces a higher pitch" (C2) — i.e. a
*cross-hand* damping, not a stroke of the striking hand.

**2.1.3 Independent stroke list from a research degree** — source C1 (Pertout), verbatim
descriptions, eight named strokes:

| Pertout name | Verbatim description |
|---|---|
| PALM | "striking the drum head with the entire hand with an emphasis on the palm, in a flat position" |
| FINGERS | "striking the drum head area with the fingertips, while resting the palm on the skin" |
| BASS | "striking and retracting the palm from the center of the drum head" |
| OPEN TONE | "striking the edge of the drum head with the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers are in an elevated closed position" |
| MUFFLED TONE | "striking the edge with the ridge of the palm, while pressing into the drum head with the extended fingers in a lowered closed position" |
| SLAP | "striking the edge with the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers in an open position simultaneously strike and grab the drum head" |
| OPEN SLAP | "striking the edge with the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers in an elevated open position strike with the tips then retract" |
| MUFFLED SLAP | slap "with the incorporation of the other hand muting the drum head" |

C1's PALM/FINGERS pair is C2's `tono de talón de mano` / `toque de dedos` — i.e. the two
halves of the heel–toe (manoteo) motion named as independent strokes. **This is the clearest
evidence in the bucket that heel and toe are strokes, not ornaments.**

### 2.2 Afro-Cuban — bongó, timbales, minor percussion

Source C5 (*Bongo drum*): the pair is `hembra` (larger, 'female') and `macho` (smaller,
'male'), joined by a wooden bridge. `martillo` ('hammer') is "a steady pattern or ostinato
of eighth-notes". The glissando is named **`bramido`** ('howl'): "done by rubbing the third
finger, supported by the thumb, across the head of the drum" — the same physical gesture
that C2 calls `tono deslizado` on the conga. Heel–toe is the martillo's engine
(round A sources; C5 does not name it).

Source C4 (*Timbales*):

| Term | Meaning (verbatim where quoted) |
|---|---|
| macho | 13 in (33 cm) drum |
| hembra | 14 in (35 cm) drum |
| paila / pailas criollas | alternative name for the instrument, from the Spanish metal cookware bowls |
| timbaleta | alternative name |
| timbalitos / pailitas | "small timbales with diameters of 15 centimetres (6 in), 20 centimetres (8 in) or 25 centimetres (10 in)" |
| cáscara | "The shells and the typical pattern played on them are referred to as cáscara" — the word names **both a site (the shell) and a pattern** |
| abanico | 'fan'; a fill, popularised in the 1930s; round A trade sources describe it as a rim-to-head sweep/flam figure — UNVERIFIED at the level of a primary definition |
| baqueteo | "The basic timbales part for danzón is called the baqueteo" |
| típico | a soloing style, "tastefully sparse, straight forward" |
| rim shot, roll | C4 lists "a variety of stick strokes, rim shots, and rolls" |

### 2.3 Indian — tabla bols

Head anatomy used by the bol definitions: `syahi` (the black tuning paste at the centre),
`maidan` (the open skin ring between syahi and rim), `kinar`/`chanti`/`chat` (the outer rim
band), `sur` (the ring between). Drums: `dayan`/`tabla` (right, treble), `bayan`/`dagga`
(left, bass).

Source I1 (Wikipedia *Tabla*), verbatim:

| Bol | Drum | Verbatim description |
|---|---|---|
| Na | dayan | "striking the edge of the syahi with the last two fingers of the right hand" |
| Ta / Ra | dayan | "striking sharply with the index finger against the rim while simultaneously applying gentle pressure to the edge of the syahi" |
| Tin | dayan | "placing the last two fingers of the right hand lightly against the syahi and striking on the border between the syahi and the maidan" |
| Te | dayan | "striking the center of the syahi with the middle finger in Delhi gharana, or using middle, ring, and little fingers together in Varanasi style" |
| Ti | dayan | "striking the center of the syahi with the index finger" |
| Tun | dayan | "striking the center of the syahi with the index finger to excite the fundamental vibration mode" |
| TheRe | dayan | "striking of syahi with palm" |
| Ghe | bayan | "holding wrist down and arching the fingers over the syahi; the middle and ring-fingers then strike the maidan" |
| Ga | bayan | "striking the index finger" |
| Ka / Ke / Kat | bayan | "striking with the flat palm and fingers" |

Source I2 (TaalGyan) gives a slightly different assignment — a real disagreement, recorded
in §4:

| Bol | Drum | Verbatim |
|---|---|---|
| Ta / Na | dayan | "hit the edge of the chanti with your index finger" |
| Tin | dayan | "hit the maidan with your index finger" |
| Tu | dayan | "hit the centre of the Syahi with the edge of your index finger" |
| Te | dayan | "hitting the syahi with your middle finger or middle finger+ring finger together" |
| Tete | dayan | Te then index finger on syahi centre |
| Ka / Ki / Ke / Kath | bayan | "hit the Syahi with the fingers stretched out" (flat hand) |
| Ga / Gha / Ge / Ghe | bayan | "hit the skin with your middle finger and ring finger" |
| Dha | both | Ga (bayan) + Ta (dayan) simultaneously |
| Dhin | both | Ga (bayan) + Tin (dayan) simultaneously |
| Tirkit | both | "Tete + Ka + Te in quick succession" |

I2 records explicitly: "The technique to play a bol varies from player to player and
gharana to gharana."

### 2.4 Indian — mridangam

Source I3 (Mridangam Stroke Dataset v1.5, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.1265188; recorded by
Akshay Anantapadmanabhan). Ten codified stroke labels, verbatim:

`Bheem, Cha, Dheem, Dhin, Num, Ta, Tha, Tham, Thi, Thom`

7 162 examples over six tonics `B, C, Csh, D, Dsh, E`; filenames
`<StrokeName>_<Tonic>_<InstanceNumber>.wav`. Citing paper: Anantapadmanabhan, Bellur,
Murthy, "Modal analysis and transcription of strokes of the mridangam using non-negative
matrix factorization", ICASSP 2013, pp. 181–185.

This is the most *machine-readable* world-percussion stroke vocabulary found in the bucket:
a closed label set with an example count per label, published under a DOI.

### 2.5 Persian — tonbak / zarb

Source M1 (Nasehpour 2003), the fullest single stroke inventory in the bucket. Persian
script as the source gives it.

| Stroke | Persian | Verbatim / description |
|---|---|---|
| tom / ton | تم / تن | bass; "played in the center of the skin", hand "partially cupped", struck "with the fingertips of the four fingers, from the pinkie to the index" |
| bak (two-fingered) | بک | treble on the rim; "played by the flesh of the first joint of the two middle and ring fingers" |
| bak (one-fingered) | بک | "played in the same way, but only by the ring finger"; softer |
| pelang / beshkan | پلنگ / بشکن | finger **snap**, "by all fingers" (pinkie, ring, middle); index-finger snap "not popular in Ostad Farhangfar's style" |
| bargardan | برگردان | Farhangfar's substitute for the index snap: index finger resting on the middle finger, striking the middle of the skin |
| riz-e-por | ریز پر | full roll, "nine-fingered", both hands' takriz combined |
| takriz | تکریز | the roll component: five fingers free hand, four fingers non-free hand |
| riz-e-shallaghi | ریز شلاقی | "combination of the both two-fingered bak of the free and the non-free hands"; zurkhaneh repertoire |
| riz-e-timpani | ریز تیمپانی | "combination of both one-fingered bak of the free and the non-free hands"; "resembles the roll produced by the drumsticks on a timpani" |
| riz-pelang | ریز پلنگ | one-fingered bak (free hand) + ring-finger snap (non-free hand) |
| eshareh / zinat | اشاره / زینت | "a combination of some strokes and is a flourish prior to (usually an accented) stroke"; "its role is like seasoning for food" |

`eshareh` is a **named grace/ornament class defined by function, not by attack count**
— see §3 and §5.

### 2.6 Arabic / Turkish — darbuka, doumbek, frame drums

Round A consensus across M7, M8 and the belly-dance teaching literature: the core set is
`doum`/`düm` (bass, centre, flat of the palm or base of fingers), `tek` (rim, fingertips,
dominant hand), `ka` (the same rim stroke played by the non-dominant hand), `slap`
(cupped palm, relaxed fingers, edge; "a louder, accented version of the tek"), plus
`finger roll` (index→little in sequence) and ghost notes. **`tek` and `ka` are the same
physical stroke distinguished only by limb** — see §4; this is the clearest
limb-encoded-in-the-name case in the bucket.

M2 (Nasehpour, citing Darvishi, During, Pahlevan, Setayeshgar) gives the frame-drum
*instrument* name field, which matters because KITWARP will have to hold these as distinct
instruments rather than as one "frame drum":

Persian `daf` (دَف, large), `dap` (دپ, pre-Islamic and Uyghur), `dayereh` (دایره, medium),
`darah` (دارَه), `dariyeh` (داریه), `dezeh`/`dizeh` (دِزِه, North Khorasan), `diyareh`
(دیاره, Gilan), `jing` (جینگ, Hormozgan/Khuzestan), `sama` (سما, Hormozgan), `tar` (طار,
Hormozgan), `zanjari` (زنجاری, Khuzestan), `zhang` (ژانگ, Baluchistan);
Turkic `al-dafi` (اَل دفی), `das-dayereh` (دس‌دایره), `def`, `tef`;
Arabic `dof` (دُف, pl. `dofuf`), `dafif` (دفیف), `mazhar` (مزهر, "bass version of riq"),
`riq` (رق, small);
elsewhere `adufe` (Portuguese/Galician), `bendir` (North African), `pandeiro` (Brazil),
`pandereta`/`pandero` (Spain, Latin America), `kanjira` (South India), `tof` (Hebrew).

Persian daf technique (Wikipedia *Daf*, M4): "the left hand, which also holds the daf,
strikes the edges, and the right hand strikes the center"; and the snap — "The right-hand
fingers are fastened about their neighbours and suddenly released, like the action of
finger-snapping, to produce loud, rapid, sharp sounds."

### 2.7 West African — djembe and dunun

Source W1 (Wikipedia *Djembe*), with measured frequency data — unusual and valuable:

| Stroke | Verbatim contact description | Frequency | Modes emphasised |
|---|---|---|---|
| bass | "striking the drum with the palm and flat fingers near the center of the skin" | 65–80 Hz | Helmholtz resonance |
| tone | "striking the drum closer to the edge; most of the area of the fingers and the edge of the palm contact the skin" | 300–420 Hz | (0,1) |
| slap | "striking the drum closer to the edge; the contact area is limited to the edge of the palm and the fingertips" | 700–1000 Hz | (2,1), (0,2), (3,1), (1,2), (0,3) |
| tonpalo / "third slap" / lé | medium-pitched sound between tone and slap | ~568 Hz | (1,1) |

French pedagogy (round A, W7) names the same three as `basse`, `tonique`, `claqué`, with
the same site rule: "La basse se joue au centre, la tonique et le claqué près du bord"
— i.e. **the three djembe strokes differ in site+position+contact, not in an abstract
"technique" label**, which is exactly the axis decomposition KITWARP proposes.

Dunun family (W1): `kenkeni` (also `kensedeni`, highest), `sangban` (medium),
`dundunba`/`dununba` (bass); Mali ensemble `konkoni`, `khassonka`. Round A: each dunun
player "strikes one side of the drum with a stick and plays a bell with the other hand"
— the bell is a **second instrument on the same layout slot**, not a site on the drum.

### 2.8 East Asian — taiko

Source E1 (Taiko Dictionary), verbatim. Drum names: `taiko` (generic), `nagadou daiko` /
`miya daiko`, `byou daiko` ("drum heads attached to the body with nails, called 'byou'"),
`o-daiko` ("the largest nagadou taiko, with a diameter over 36 inches"), `chu daiko`,
`ko daiko` ("10 to 17 inches"), `wadaiko`/`jozuke` ("18 to 24 inches"), `shime daiko` /
`tsukeshime daiko` ("the small high pitched taiko … that often plays the 'jiuchi' of a
song"), `eitetsu okedo daiko`/`kakko`, `hira daiko` ("cut to a quarter of the height of a
standard taiko"), `okedo taiko`/`oke daiko`, `uchiwa daiko` ("a 'fan' drum").

**Playing-surface terms — a direct site vocabulary:**
`hara` = "the center of the skin"; `fuchi` = "the edge of the top and bottom of the drum".
`tekoto` = "a style of playing where the drummer alternately plays the 'hara' and the
'fuchi' of the taiko".

**Kuchi-shōga table (E1), by instrument column:**

| Taiko | Shimedaiko | Atarigane | Verbatim definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Don (Kon) | Ten | Chan | "A single loud beat to the center (hara) of the drum" |
| Do (Ko, Ro) | Te (Ke, Re) | Chi (Ki) | "A single firm beat to the hara, but with a value 1/2 that of 'don'" |
| DoKo | TeKe | ChiKi | "2 Fast beats of equal sound, and power" |
| DoRo | TeRe | ChiRi | "2 Fast beats, but with a slight 'rolling' feel" |
| Tsu | Tsu | Tsu | "A note played softly" |
| TsuKu | TsuKu | TsuKu | "2 Fast beats played softly" |
| Ka (Ta) | Ka | — | "A beat played on the edge of the drum (fuchi)" |
| KaRa | KaRa | — | "2 Fast beats played on the fuchi, with a slight 'rolling' feel" |
| Su | Su | Su | "A rest" |
| Zu | Zu | — | "Another term for a soft beat" |

E2 (Wikipedia) adds the **hand encoding**: "Some taiko groups use 'don,' 'do,' 'tsu,' and
'ka' to represent a hit with the right hand, and 'kon,' 'ko,' 'ku,' and 'ra' to represent a
left-hand hit", and warns "kuchi shōga does not have a fixed vocabulary".

Playing-position style names (E1) — these are *postures*, and fit no KITWARP axis:
`beta uchi`, `kagami uchi`, `hachijo daiko`, `miyake daiko`, `onbayashi`, `sukeroku`,
`ka kai e`, plus `oroshi` (an accelerating opening figure).

### 2.9 East Asian — janggu, Beijing opera, kendang

Source E4 (*Janggu*): body parts `jorongmok` ("the round tube in the middle connecting the
left and right side of the hourglass-shaped body"), `gungpyeon` (left head, thick hide, deep
tone), `chaepyeon` (right head, light hide, high tone). Beaters: `gungchae` ("shaped like a
mallet with a round head", bamboo and hardwood), `yeolchae` ("always made from bamboo").
Strokes: `kung`/`gung` (gungpyeon), `deok` and `ta`/`tta` (chaepyeon), `deong` (both heads
struck together). Round A adds `gi` — "lightly striking the end" — and `gideok` /
`gigideok` as compound figures (UNVERIFIED: not yet confirmed from a primary source).

Source E7 (CompMusic): Beijing opera percussion instruments are `bangu` ("Ban+danpigu:
clapper-drum"), `naobo` ("Qibo+Danao: Cymbals"), `daluo` ("Big gong"), `xiaoluo`
("Small gong"). The oral-notation system is `luogujing`; round A describes it as an
onomatopoeic syllable system in which **each syllable names an instrument combination**
rather than a stroke on one instrument — a genuinely different mapping from every other
tradition in this bucket. The syllable inventory itself is NOT YET OBTAINED (E8 is the
paper that should carry it).

Source E9 (*Kendang*): Javanese drum names `kendhang ageng`/`gede` ("the largest kendang,
which usually has the deepest tone"), `kendhang wayang` (medium), `kendhang batangan` /
`ciblon` ("used for the most complex or lively rhythms"), `kendhang ketipung` ("the smallest
kendang"). The article gives only one stroke string, for kendang Reog: "dang thak dhak thung
glhang". The systematic Javanese/Balinese stroke syllabary is NOT OBTAINED from this source.

### 2.10 Brazilian

Source B2 (*Surdo*): `primeira`/`marcação` (largest, deepest, 22–26 in), `segunda`/
`resposta`/`respondor` (medium, 20–22 in), `terceira`/`cutador` ('cutter', smallest,
14–18 in). B2 names no strokes; it records only "Surdo drummers beat the drums using hard
or soft mallets" and that in samba-reggae the middle surdos are played with two mallets.
The open/`abafado` (muffled) distinction is universal in the teaching literature but is
NOT VERIFIED from a source of authority B or better yet.

Source B1 (*Pandeiro*): parts `drumhead`, `rim`, `platinelas` ("cupped metal jingles on the
rim"). Techniques given only as a sequence: "alternating the thumb, fingertips, heel, and
palm of the hand"; plus shake and "run a finger along the head to produce a drum roll".
Portuguese pedagogical notation legend recovered in round A but not yet from a citable
source: `G` = polegar (thumb), `P` = palma da mão (palm), `D` = ponta dos dedos
(fingertips), `T` = tapa (slap) — treat as UNVERIFIED until B3 is read.
