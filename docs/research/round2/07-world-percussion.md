# Round 2 · Bucket 07 — World and regional percussion nomenclature

Scope: the codified stroke and instrument vocabularies of Afro-Cuban, Brazilian, Indian
(Hindustani and Carnatic), Arabic / Turkish / Persian, West African, East Asian and cajón
traditions. Method per `.agents/round2/BRIEF.md`: round A breadth (20 searches, English,
Spanish, Portuguese, French, German and transliterated source languages, scholarly through
pedagogical registers), round B depth, round C self-critique.

**Headline result.** Fourteen independent traditions on four continents, none of which
borrowed from the drum kit, converge on the same small stroke set: a low centre stroke, a
high edge stroke, a damped variant of each, a slap, and a heel/tip pair. The sources define
these strokes *physically* — by where on the instrument, how far from the centre, which
part of the hand, and whether contact is held — and not as atomic named techniques. That
is the KITWARP axis decomposition arrived at independently by centuries of pedagogy. §3
maps every term; §3.4 lists the eleven kinds of term that fit no axis, which is where the
model is actually incomplete.

## 2. Extracted terminology (round B)

### 2.1 Afro-Cuban — conga / tumbadora

**2.1.1 Drum names by size and role** — C2 (size table), C3.

| Name | Head diameter | Role |
|---|---|---|
| supertumba / rebajador | 14 in (36 cm) | largest |
| tumba / salidor | 12–12.5 in (30–32 cm) | lowest pitched; in rumba the marking drum |
| conga / tres dos | 11.5–12 in (29–30 cm) | middle |
| quinto | ~11 in (28 cm) | lead, highest; "constantly punctuating, coloring, and accenting" (C2) |
| requinto | <10 in (25 cm) | smaller lead |
| requinto chico | 9 in (23 cm) | smallest; "played while hanging from a shoulder strap" (C2) |

C2 also records a second, incompatible naming convention: in *conjuntos* playing son cubano,
in charangas and wherever one or two congas complement other instruments, "the drums are
named like the bongos: *macho* (male) and *hembra* (female), for the higher and
lower-pitched drums, respectively", with an optional *tercera*. C3 gives the rumba trio as
`quinto` (lead, highest) / `tres dos` (middle) / `tumba` or `salidor` (lowest).

**2.1.2 Stroke set, Spanish nomenclature** — C2, verbatim.

| Spanish | English | Physical description |
|---|---|---|
| tono abierto | open tone | "played with the four fingers near the rim of the head, producing a clear resonant sound with a higher pitch than muffled and bass tones" |
| tono ahogado / apagado | muffled tone | "like the open tone, it is made by striking the drum with the four fingers, but holding the fingers against the head to muffle the tone" |
| tono seco abierto | open slap | slap, fingers rebound |
| tono seco cerrado (*tapao*, *galleta*, *quemao*) | closed slap | slap, fingers stay on the head |
| tono seco presionado | pressed slap | slap with sustained pressure |
| tono bajo de palma | bass tone | "played with the full palm, in a slightly cupped position, somewhat off center on the head. It produces a low muted sound" |
| tono de talón de mano | heel tone | heel of the palm |
| toque de dedos | fingers tone | fingers |
| toque de punta de dedos | fingertips tone | fingertips |
| tono de uñas | fingernails tone | "played with the tip of the nails, usually finger by finger in quick succession, starting with the pinky" |
| tono deslizado | glissando, "moose call" | "done by rubbing the third finger, supported by the thumb, across the head of the drum. The finger is sometimes moistened with saliva or sweat" |

`manoteo` = the rocking alternation of palm-heel and fingertips that produces the conga
equivalent of a roll (round-A Spanish sources: "movimiento de balancín entre el talón de la
mano y la punta de los dedos"). The muted slap is made by "playing an open tone while the
other hand rests on the drumhead, which produces a higher pitch" (C2) — **cross-hand**
damping, not damping by the striking hand.

**2.1.3 Independent stroke list from a research degree** — C1 (Pertout), verbatim, eight
strokes:

| Pertout name | Verbatim description |
|---|---|
| PALM | "striking the drum head with the entire hand with an emphasis on the palm, in a flat position" |
| FINGERS | "striking the drum head area with the fingertips, while resting the palm on the skin" |
| BASS | "striking and retracting the palm from the center of the drum head" |
| OPEN TONE | "striking the edge of the drum head with the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers are in an elevated closed position" |
| MUFFLED TONE | "striking the edge with the ridge of the palm, while pressing into the drum head with the extended fingers in a lowered closed position" |
| SLAP | "striking the edge with the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers in an open position simultaneously strike and grab the drum head" |
| OPEN SLAP | "striking the edge with the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers in an elevated open position strike with the tips then retract" |
| MUFFLED SLAP | slap "with the incorporation of the other hand muting the drum head" |

C1's PALM / FINGERS pair is C2's `tono de talón de mano` / `toque de dedos`: the two halves
of the heel–toe (`manoteo`) motion, named as independent strokes by a source that is not
describing a roll. **This is the bucket's clearest evidence that heel and toe are strokes,
not ornaments** — which is how v0.1 already has them (`technique` 13 `heel`, 14 `toe`),
though there they are foot terms (§4.6).

### 2.2 Afro-Cuban — bongó, timbales, minor percussion

C5 (*Bongo drum*): the pair is `hembra` (larger, 'female') and `macho` (smaller, 'male'),
joined by a wooden bridge. `martillo` ('hammer') is "a steady pattern or ostinato of
eighth-notes". The glissando has its own name here — **`bramido`** ('howl'), "done by
rubbing the third finger, supported by the thumb, across the head of the drum": the same
physical gesture C2 calls `tono deslizado` on the conga, under a different name.

C4 (*Timbales*):

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| macho | 13 in (33 cm) drum |
| hembra | 14 in (35 cm) drum |
| paila / pailas criollas | alternative name for the instrument, after the Spanish metal cookware bowls |
| timbaleta | alternative name |
| timbalitos / pailitas | "small timbales with diameters of 15 centimetres (6 in), 20 centimetres (8 in) or 25 centimetres (10 in)" |
| cáscara | "The shells and the typical pattern played on them are referred to as cáscara" — the word names **both the site and the pattern** |
| abanico | 'fan'; a fill popularised in the 1930s |
| baqueteo | "The basic timbales part for danzón is called the baqueteo" |
| típico | a soloing style, "tastefully sparse, straight forward" |
| rim shot, roll | C4: "a variety of stick strokes, rim shots, and rolls" |

**2.2.1 `cáscara`, `paila`, `abanico` — the three terms referred to this bucket by the
vendor-glossary worker, which could find no citable text because Latin Percussion publishes
technique only as video.** Text definitions exist. All three are closed below.

**`cáscara` — closed, authority A/B.** C13: Marc Dicciani (Director, School of Music,
University of the Arts, Philadelphia), *Intro to Afro-Cuban Mambo Rhythms and Drumset
Patterns*, © 2009, p. 1, `http://www.dicciani.com/materials/afro_cuban_intro_and_basics.pdf`.
Verbatim:

> "The cascara or 'shell' pattern is a very commonly heard rhythm in Afro-Cuban styles. It is
> called cascara because it is often played on the shells of the timbales or on the rim of
> the floor tom on the drum set during soft sections of an arrangement. It is also sometimes
> referred to as **palito** which means 'little sticks' because it is sometimes played on the
> **gua gua**, a mounted piece of bamboo. Today, cascara refers more to the rhythm itself
> than to the drum on which it is played."

This is the decisive sentence for the pivot: *"cascara refers more to the rhythm itself than
to the drum on which it is played."* The word began as a site (`site.shell`) and has become
a pattern name. A vendor articulation labelled "cascara" therefore asserts a **groove**, not
a stroke, and must not be minted as a pivot term; what it implies about the stroke is only
(site = shell, implement = stick). The same page supplies `palito` ('little sticks') and
`gua gua` (a mounted bamboo idiophone) as the two alternative sites for the identical
pattern, and names two clave positions, `bombo` (2nd note of the 3-side) and `ponche` (3rd
note of the 3-side).

**`paila` — closed, but the word carries two incompatible senses.**
(a) *The instrument.* C4: `pailas` / `pailas criollas` is an alternative name for the
timbales, after the Spanish metal cookware bowls. Spanish Wikipedia (C14,
`https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbales`) verbatim: "los **timbales**, *pailas, timbaletas*
o *tarolas tropicales;* en la música latina (salsa, por ejemplo), tambores cilíndricos de un
solo parche, con armazón de metal, más cortos que los tom toms, y afinados más agudos".
(b) *The shell.* C15 (`https://rhythmnotes.net/how-to-play-timbales/`, authority C) uses
`paila` for "the shell of the drum" — i.e. as a synonym for `cáscara` in its site sense.
A converter meeting a note named `paila` cannot tell from the name alone whether it means
the instrument or the surface; see §4.1.

**`abanico` — closed at authority C, physically specific.** C15 verbatim: it is "the second
fill", it "signals the transition between quieter dynamic sections and the louder dynamic
sections", and it consists of **"a rimshot, followed by a roll on beat four"** on the
**macho** drum, the roll being "usually either a 7-stroke roll or a 9-stroke roll" and
resolving with a rimshot on beat one. The initiating rimshot falls on the *and* of beat
three in 2:3 clave and on the downbeat of beat three in 3:2. C4 dates its popularisation to
the 1930s.

Decomposed: `abanico` = rimshot → measured roll → rimshot, on one named drum, with a
structural signalling function. It is therefore **a two-term figure plus a cue, not a
stroke**: `technique.rimshot` + `ornament.roll` + `technique.rimshot`. Round A's
description of it as "a flam-like sweep from rim to head" is *not* supported by any text
source reached and should be treated as wrong rather than merely unverified. The
authority is C, so mark the stroke sequence UNVERIFIED-AT-PRIMARY; but it is a text
definition of a specific physical gesture, which is what was asked for.

**`baqueteo` — bonus, and it settles the notation.** Wikipedia *Danzón* (C16,
`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danz%C3%B3n`): "The basic timbales part for danzón is called
the baqueteo", and its notated example uses "slashed noteheads [to] indicate muted drum
strokes, and the regular noteheads [to] indicate open strokes", with the güiro doubling the
same pattern. The baqueteo is thus an **open/muted alternation on the head**, which maps to
`damping` — not to a site, and not to a stroke name of its own.

Minor percussion (round-A Spanish sources, authority C, treat as UNVERIFIED pending C11):
`güiro` played with "long and short sounds, made by scraping up and down in long or short
strokes"; `chekeré` / `ágbe` / `aggüé` "played by shaking, striking, and rotating";
`clave` two struck sticks; `maracas` shaken.

### 2.3 Indian — tabla bols

Head anatomy used by every bol definition: `syahi` (the black tuning paste at the centre),
`maidan` (the open skin ring between syahi and rim), `kinar` / `chanti` / `chat` (the outer
band), `sur` (the intermediate ring). Drums: `dayan` / `tabla` (right, treble), `bayan` /
`dagga` (left, bass).

I1 (Wikipedia *Tabla*), verbatim:

| Bol | Drum | Verbatim description |
|---|---|---|
| Na | dayan | "striking the edge of the syahi with the last two fingers of the right hand" |
| Ta / Ra | dayan | "striking sharply with the index finger against the rim while simultaneously applying gentle pressure to the edge of the syahi" |
| Tin | dayan | "placing the last two fingers of the right hand lightly against the syahi and striking on the border between the syahi and the maidan" |
| Te | dayan | "striking the center of the syahi with the middle finger in Delhi gharana, or using middle, ring, and little fingers together in Varanasi style" |
| Ti | dayan | "striking the center of the syahi with the index finger" |
| Tun | dayan | "striking the center of the syahi with the index finger to excite the fundamental vibration mode" |
| TheRe | dayan | "striking of syahi with palm" |
| Ghe | bayan | "holding wrist down and arching the fingers over the syahi; the middle and ring-fingers then strike the maidan" |
| Ga | bayan | "striking the index finger" |
| Ka / Ke / Kat | bayan | "striking with the flat palm and fingers" |

I2 (TaalGyan) assigns some of the same syllables differently — a real disagreement, §4.3:

| Bol | Drum | Verbatim |
|---|---|---|
| Ta / Na | dayan | "hit the edge of the chanti with your index finger" |
| Tin | dayan | "hit the maidan with your index finger" |
| Tu | dayan | "hit the centre of the Syahi with the edge of your index finger" |
| Te | dayan | "hitting the syahi with your middle finger or middle finger+ring finger together" |
| Tete | dayan | Te, then index finger on the syahi centre |
| Ka / Ki / Ke / Kath | bayan | "hit the Syahi with the fingers stretched out" (flat hand) |

I2 states the reason outright: "The technique to play a bol varies from player to player and
gharana to gharana."

**2.3.1 The four-way acoustic reduction — I4, ISMIR 2021, Table 1, verbatim.** This is the
single most axis-shaped source in the bucket: it factors every common bol into
(damped | resonant | none) *per drum* and then names the kit equivalent.

| Category | Bols | Bass (bayan) | Treble (dayan) | Drumkit equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D** damped | Ti-Ta, Te-Re, Tak, Ke, Tra, Kda | D / Nil | D / Nil | HH |
| **RT** resonant treble | Na, Tin, Tun, Din | D / Nil | R | SD |
| **RB** resonant bass | Ghe, Dhe, Dhi, Dhet | R | D / Nil | BD |
| **B** resonant both | Dha, Dhin | R | R | BD + SD |

I4 §1, verbatim: "An important taxonomic level for tabla sounds is based on which of the
two drums is struck and the manner of striking, giving rise to the three classes: resonant
treble (right drum), resonant bass (left drum), and damped (either drum) … the specific
manner gives rise to either a damped stroke with a sharp and short-duration sound or a
pitched (resonant) stroke with ringing sound, **which can further be pitch modulated in the
case of the left drum**." I4 also records the size of the vocabulary: "a set of between
10-20 distinct tabla bols (depending on playing style) found in practice".

### 2.4 Indian — mridangam, ghatam, kanjira

**I3, Mridangam Stroke Dataset v1.5** (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.1265188; performed and recorded
by Akshay Anantapadmanabhan). Ten codified stroke labels, verbatim:

`Bheem, Cha, Dheem, Dhin, Num, Ta, Tha, Tham, Thi, Thom`

7 162 examples across six tonics `B, C, Csh, D, Dsh, E`; filenames
`<StrokeName>_<Tonic>_<InstanceNumber>.wav`. Citing paper: Anantapadmanabhan, Bellur,
Murthy, "Modal analysis and transcription of strokes of the mridangam using non-negative
matrix factorization", ICASSP 2013, pp. 181–185. This is the most machine-readable
world-percussion stroke vocabulary found in the bucket: a closed label set with an example
count per label, under a DOI.

**I8, the `karya` sequencer's mridangam module** — an independent, executable codification
that splits by head first and then by contact:

- top type `Stroke` = `Thoppi` (left hand) | `Valantalai` (right hand) | `Both`
- `Thoppi`: `Tha` (Palm | Fingertips | Fingers-flat), `Thom` (Low = open or gumiki, Up =
  gumiki), `Gum` = "Just the gumiki movement, no strike. Or possibly a light strike to make
  it speak if it doesn't sustain."
- `Valantalai`: `Ki`, `Ta`, `Mi` = "light Ki, played with middle finger", `Nam`, `Din`,
  `AraiChapu` = "half chapu", `MuruChapu` = "full chapu, pinky on saddam", `Dheem`,
  `Kin`, `Tan` (meetu region)

`gumiki` is the left-head pitch bend produced by pressing and sliding the heel of the hand.
It is a **modulation**, not a stroke: `Gum` is defined as the movement *with no strike*.

**I9, ghatam** — a clay pot with no membrane, whose vocabulary is entirely site-and-contact:
"Loud bass tones can be produced if one hits with the flat hand the opening at the top";
"fingers, thumbs, palms, and heels of the hands to strike its outer surface"; "striking the
body of the Ghatam by the lower parts of the wrists"; "Sometimes the ghatam is turned around
so that the mouth faces towards the audience and the performer plays on the neck"; the
mouth is held against the abdomen and the tone varied "by changing the distance between the
pot and the stomach".

**I10, kanjira** — "The fingertips of the left hand can be used to bend the pitch by
applying pressure near the outer rim"; "normally played with the palm and fingers of the
right hand, while the left hand supports the drum"; and the tuning practice "the performer
reduces the tension of the drumhead by sprinkling water on the inside of the instrument".

### 2.6 Arabic / Turkish — goblet drum and frame drums

M4 (*Goblet drum*), verbatim, with M8 corroborating:

| Stroke | Verbatim |
|---|---|
| doom / düm | "the deeper bass sound produced by striking the head near the center with the length of the fingers and palm and taking off the hand for an open sound" |

| ka | "a secondary-hand variation of the 'tak'" |

### 2.7 West African — djembe, dunun, sabar

W1 (*Djembe*), with measured frequency and modal data:

| Stroke | Verbatim contact description | Frequency | Modes emphasised |
|---|---|---|---|
| bass | "striking the drum with the palm and flat fingers near the center of the skin" | 65–80 Hz | Helmholtz resonance |
| tone | "striking the drum closer to the edge; most of the area of the fingers and the edge of the palm contact the skin" | 300–420 Hz | (0,1) |
| slap | "striking the drum closer to the edge; the contact area is limited to the edge of the palm and the fingertips" | 700–1000 Hz | (2,1), (0,2), (3,1), (1,2), (0,3) |
| tonpalo / "third slap" / lé | medium-pitched sound between tone and slap | ~568 Hz | (1,1) |

Note what W1's own wording proves: `tone` and `slap` share site (near the edge) and differ
**only in contact area** — fingers-plus-palm-edge against palm-edge-plus-fingertips. French
pedagogy (W7) names the same three `basse`, `tonique`, `claqué` with the same site rule:
"La basse se joue au centre, la tonique et le claqué près du bord."

W2 (*Dunun*): `dundunba` / `dununba` (largest, lowest), `sangban` (medium), `kenkeni`
(smallest, highest, also `kensedeni`); `konkoni` = "two dunums played in Bamako style
without bells"; `khassonka dunun` = the Bamana name for the dundunba. Bell: `kenken`.
Beaters, verbatim: "a plain straight stick, curved stick with flat head (similar to the
stick used for a tama), or a straight stick with a cylindrical head attached at right angles
near one end." W2 names no individual strokes. Each dunun player strikes the head with a
stick in one hand and the mounted bell with a rod in the other: the bell is a **second
instrument on one layout slot**, not a site on the drum.

W5 (*Sabar*): drum family "n'der, thiol, goron, m'beng m'beng, toungoné, and xiin"; the
stick is the **`galan`**, "made of tamarind wood"; "The drum is generally played with one
hand and one stick or 'galan'". W4 (Utrecht) states that "Playing the sabar involves at
least nine distinct strokes: hand strokes, stick strokes, or their combinations" and that
"Each drum stroke has a name, even the smallest variation" — **but neither reached source
enumerates them.** This is the largest single hole in the bucket (§6).

### 2.9 East Asian — janggu, Beijing opera, gamelan

**E4 (*Janggu*).** Body: `jorongmok` = "the round tube in the middle connecting the left and

| 8 | "Movimento com o polegar friccionando a pele, produzindo um som continuo de platinelas agudo." | thumb **friction** on the head (= *rulo*) |
| 9 | "Movimento de raspagem da pele com as unhas, produzindo um som curto agudo." | **fingernail scrape**, head |
| 10 | "Movimento de rodar a platinela, produzindo um som agudo continuo." | **rotating the jingles** |
| 11 | "Movimento de percutir a platinela com a ponta dos dedos, produzindo um som médio curto." | **striking the jingles** with fingertips |
| 12 | "Movimento de raspagem da pele com as unhas em movimento circular, produzindo um som contínuo agudo." | **circular** fingernail scrape, head |

B3 §1.3, verbatim on the notation itself, quoting Gianesella 2012: "Stasi propõe uma grafia
bastante sintética que facilita a leitura, ao mesmo tempo em que identifica todas as
diferentes articulações propostas por ele, utilizando apenas uma linha" — a single-line
staff carrying the full articulation set, which is precisely a pivot-term encoding.
B3 figures 07–13 give the graphical rules: thumb notated below the line; base of the hand
with the stem touching the line; fingers (index, middle, ring) with the stem not touching;
flat hand at the centre of the head as an `X` above the line; `rulo` as `tr` above the line;
and `Polegar Solto` (open thumb) vs `Polegar Abafado` (muted thumb) as separate glyphs.
B3 p. 40 adds `rimshot` = "toque do dedo indicador ou polegar entre a pele e o aro do
instrumento", `rulo de mão` = "movimento de rotação de pulso rápido, atingindo a pele com
polegar e dedos", `rulo` = "fricção entre o polegar ou dedo do meio na pele do instrumento
simulando um trinado", and `tapas` = "toque de mão espalmada na pele".

B3 also records the historical shift in the *ordering* of the strokes, quoting Potts 2012
on Marcos Suzano: "Whereas the samba-choro pattern always began with a thumb stroke, (and
indeed, most all traditional patterns led with the lower portion of the hand), Suzano
reversed this concept and started his grooves by leading with his fingertips", creating a
sound named "grave com a ponta dos dedos". And, quoting Barbosa 2015, the explicit
kit-mapping: thumb on the head = bumbo (kick), fingertips at the centre = caixa (snare),
jingle movement = pratos (cymbals).

**B2 (*Surdo*).** `primeira` / `marcação` (largest, deepest, 22–26 in); `segunda` /
`resposta` / `respondor` (medium, 20–22 in); `terceira` / `cutador` ('cutter', smallest,
14–18 in). Beaters given only generically: "Surdo drummers beat the drums using hard or soft
mallets"; in samba-reggae "the middle surdos are played with two mallets". The
open / `abafado` (muffled) distinction is universal in the teaching literature but is **NOT
VERIFIED** here from a source of authority B or better.

**B6 (*Tamborim*).** Beater: "a beater made of several nylon or polyacetal threads bound
together" (in samba-batucada); otherwise "a small wooden drumstick", occasionally fingers or
the rim. `virado`, verbatim: "the drum is flipped upside-down in the middle of the pattern,
which produces one note that is slightly delayed". `carreteiro` = "the main groove pattern".

**B7 (*Cuíca*).** "The cuíca has a wooden or bamboo stick fastened at one end inside the
drum in the center of, and perpendicular to, the drumhead." "This stick is rosined and
rubbed with a cloth", "rubbed up and down with a wet cloth held in one hand". Pitch:
"Changing the pressure on the head of the drum from the outside produces the different
pitches and timbres", using "the fingers of the other hand to press down on the skin of the
drum near the place where the stick is attached." No onomatopoeic sound names given.

### 2.11 Cajón — flamenco, Peruvian, Cuban

**X1 (Piulestán Nieto, *Revista Centro de Investigación Flamenco Telethusa* 13(15): 26–33,
2020), verbatim Spanish:**

| Golpe | Verbatim |
|---|---|
| agudos | "en la parte superior de la tapa" |
| golpes de relleno | "de poca intensidad" |
| chasqueos o rotos | "con intensidad" |
| apagados | "menos roto que el anterior" |
| graves | "golpeando con la mano en el centro del cajón" |
| grave-agudo | "ejecutando un doble golpe con una sola mano, la palma para obtener el sonido grave y las yemas el agudo" |

X1 further records strikes "con el talón del pie sobre los laterales del cajón" and "con los
nudillos y dedos sobre la madera", plus optional `escobillas` (brushes). No notation legend.

`grave-agudo` deserves emphasis: **one hand, two attacks, two different pitches, one name**
— the cajón's heel–toe, and a direct parallel to the conga `manoteo`.

**X2 (*Cajón*).** The striking face is the **`tapa`**; "A sound hole is cut on the back
side"; "Sheets of 13 to 19 mm … thick wood are generally used for five sides of the box. A
thinner sheet of plywood is nailed on as the sixth side." Regional types: Peruvian cajón,
Spanish flamenco cajón, Cuban `cajón de rumba`, Mexican `cajón de tapeo`. Playing position:
"The player sits astride the box, tilting it at an angle while striking the head between
their knees"; "The percussionist can play the sides with the top of their palms and fingers
for additional sounds". C3 records that in Cuban rumba "Cajones (wooden boxes) were used as
drums until the early 20th century, when they were replaced by tumbadoras".

### 2.12 Cross-family scholarly register

**X4 (Hornbostel & Sachs 1914).** The Würzburg file is the introductory essay only; the
numbered class list is not in it. What it does supply verbatim is the classification
*logic*, which is a decomposition into successive facets and is the pre-MIDI ancestor of the
axis idea: a glockenspiel is `1` Idiophon → struck, so `11` **Schlagidiophone** → struck
directly, `111` → `1112` **Aufschlagidiophone** → `11124` Aufschlaggefäße → `111242` Glocken
→ `1112422` Glockenspiele → `11124222` Hängeglockenspiele → `111242222`
Klöppelglockenspiele, written `111.242.222`. The essay states the intent explicitly: "man
erkennt am Stellenwert der letzten Ziffer unmittelbar die logische Rangordnung des
ausgedrückten Begriffs im System", and notes that class-wide common features exist "z. B.
für Membranophone die Art der Fellbefestigung". The German playing-action stems
`Schlag-` (struck), `Aufschlag-` (percussion on), `Gegenschlag-` (concussion), `Reib-`
(friction), `Schrap-` (scraped), `Schüttel-` (shaken) are the register the brief asked for;
the full numbered list belongs to bucket 03 and is not re-derived here.

**X6 (Hughes, via E7).** `bol` (Hindustani), `solkattu` and `konnakōl` (Carnatic),
`kuchi shōga` (Japan), `luógǔjīng` (Beijing opera), Korean onomatopoeic jangdan syllables,
and — E7 notes — Turkish and Javanese equivalents, are one phenomenon:
**acoustic-iconic mnemonic systems**. If KITWARP ever needs a family name for the class of
term in this bucket, this is the term the literature already uses.

### 3.3 What the mapping proves about the model

1. **The axis decomposition is not a modelling convenience; it is how the primary sources
   define their own terms.** W1 defines djembe `tone` and `slap` as differing *only* in
   contact area at the same site. B3's entire appendix is (contact × site × damping).
   I4's tabla table is (drum × resonant-or-damped) with the bol name as the *output*, not
   the input. E1's kuchi-shōga is (site × dynamic × attack count).
2. **Onomatopoeia converges on the axis tuple, not on the name.** `doum` / `dum` / `don` /
   `tom` / `thom` / `dang` / `dha` all denote (head, centre, palm, undamped); `tek` / `tak` /
   `te` / `ti` / `ka` / `thak` all denote (head, perimeter, fingertips). Four language
   families, one physical pair. The onomatopoeic syllable is best read as a *rendering* of
   the tuple, not as a term to be minted.
3. **The traditions that carry a machine-readable label set (I3, I4, E7) each reduce
   their own vocabulary to a smaller acoustic one when they need it to be operational** —
   I4 reduces 10–20 bols to 4 classes; E7 reduces dozens of syllables to 5 groups. Whatever
   pivot KITWARP mints for these families will face the same pressure.

### 3.4 Terms that fit NO axis — the most valuable output

Eleven kinds. Each is named by at least two independent traditions, so none is an artefact
of one source.

**(1) Ensemble-combination names — a term for a simultaneous multi-instrument sound.**
E7's luógǔjīng syllables are the pure case: `cāng` = daluo + optional naobo + optional
xiaoluo; `qiē` = naobo + xiaoluo. E7, verbatim: "the syllables that form a pattern refer to
the ensemble as a whole, and not to particular instruments." The tabla compound bols are
the same thing in miniature: `Dha` = Ga(bayan) + Ta(dayan), and I4 maps it to `BD+SD`.
Janggu `deong` = both heads struck together. **No KITWARP axis holds "these N instruments
sound at once as one named event."** A layout can express it as two simultaneous slots, but
then the *name* is lost, and in Beijing opera the name is the whole notation.

**(2) Pitch bend / pressure modulation while sounding.** mridangam `gumiki` (I8 defines
`Gum` as "just the gumiki movement, no strike"); tabla bayan modulation (I4: "which can
further be pitch modulated in the case of the left drum"); kanjira ("bend the pitch by
applying pressure near the outer rim"); cuíca ("Changing the pressure on the head of the
drum from the outside produces the different pitches"); conga `tono deslizado` / bongó
`bramido` / "moose call". Five traditions. v0.1 has `technique.gliss` (id 21), which names
a *sliding stroke*, but not a **continuous pitch state or bend depth**, and not a
modulation applied with no strike at all. This is a candidate axis, or at minimum a
controller alongside `strike_position`.

**(3) Friction as a sound-producing action, distinct from scrape.** cuíca (rosined internal
stick rubbed with a wet cloth); pandeiro `rulo` ("fricção entre o polegar ou dedo do meio na
pele"); conga glissando (moistened finger). Hornbostel–Sachs has the stem for it —
`Reibtrommel`, friction drum — as a class separate from `Schrapidiophon` (scraped). v0.1
has `scrape` (19) and `swirl` (17) but no friction-rub. A güiro scrape and a cuíca rub are
not the same action.

**(4) Instrument-orientation change as an articulation.** tamborim `virado`: "the drum is
flipped upside-down in the middle of the pattern, which produces one note that is slightly
delayed" (B6). ghatam: "the ghatam is turned around so that the mouth faces towards the
audience" (I9), and tone varied "by changing the distance between the pot and the stomach".
Fits no axis. It is not site, position, contact, technique or damping — it is a change to
the instrument's pose.

**(5) Ensemble role, as distinct from instance ordinal.** `quinto` (lead) vs `salidor`
(marking) vs `tres dos`; `sangban` ("defines the rhythm") vs `dununba` ("depth") vs
`kenkeni` ("holds the rhythm together"); surdo `marcação` vs `resposta` vs `cutador`;
Balinese `polos` (on-beat) vs `sangsih` (off-beat); `macho` / `hembra`. The brief puts
`instance` on the layout slot, "1-based ordinal, toms high to low in pitch". That captures
the *pitch order* but throws away the *function*, and in these traditions the function is
the identity: a quinto that is not soloing is still the quinto. Note also that `macho` /
`hembra` is a **gendered pair, not an ordinal** — there is no third position in the series,
which is why C2 has to add `tercera` as an afterthought.

**(6) Playing posture.** taiko `beta uchi`, `kagami uchi`, `hachijo daiko`, `miyake daiko`,
`onbayashi`, `sukeroku`, `ka kai e`; cajón "sits astride the box, tilting it at an angle";
kanjira "the left hand supports the drum". These change the sound (a drum on the floor
sounds different from one on a slanted stand) but are not strokes.

**(7) Named patterns, not sounds.** `martillo`, `cáscara` (as pattern), `baqueteo`,
`carreteiro`, `theka`, `tirkit` (borderline — a three-attack figure), `jangdan`, `kotekan`,
`kotekan empat` / `telu` / `njog cak`, `oncang-oncang`, `luógǔjīng` pattern names (~90),
`abanico`, `típico`, `oroshi`, `angsel`, `vacunao`, `palito`. These belong in whatever layer
KITWARP uses for grooves, not in the pivot vocabulary — but `cáscara` and `abanico` in
particular are shipped as articulation names by sample libraries, so the reconciliation pass
will meet them as if they were strokes. §2.2.1 shows what each actually implies about the
stroke: `cáscara` implies only (site = shell, implement = stick); `abanico` implies
rimshot + roll + rimshot on one named drum; `baqueteo` implies an open/muted alternation
and so belongs to `damping`.

**(8) A finger snap on the drum head.** tonbak `pelang` / `beshkan`, and its Farhangfar
substitute `bargardan`; daf's "fingers … fastened about their neighbours and suddenly
released, like the action of finger-snapping". v0.1 `technique` has no `snap`. It is not a
slap (no palm contact), not a hit, not an ornament (it is a single primary attack).

**(9) Cross-hand damping — damping by the hand that is not striking.** conga muffled slap
("the other hand rests on the drumhead"); tabla `Dha` where the bayan hand's state modifies
the dayan stroke; kanjira left-hand pressure. v0.1's `damping` description already notes
"hand damping while striking and while not striking are different sounds", so the axis
anticipates this — but the value list (`none, muted, damped, towel, gated`) has no value
that says *which* hand damps. In hand-drum traditions that distinction changes the pitch,
not just the decay.

**(10) School / lineage as a qualifier on a stroke.** tabla `Te` is "the middle finger in
Delhi gharana, or … middle, ring, and little fingers together in Varanasi style" (I1);
I2: "varies from player to player and gharana to gharana"; tonbak `bargardan` exists because
the index snap is "not popular in Ostad Farhangfar's style" (M1); E2: "kuchi shōga does not
have a fixed vocabulary". The same *name* denotes different physical strokes depending on
the school. v0.1 has no axis for provenance-of-technique, and `voicing` is about the
recorded sound, not the lineage. Any world-percussion pivot term will need either a lineage
qualifier or a documented decision to pick one school and alias the rest.

**(11) Rest and silence as notated members of the stroke vocabulary.** taiko `Su` = "a
rest"; E2's fricative and semivowel rest syllables `sa`, `ho`, `iya`. Every oral mnemonic
system in the bucket carries silence as a named member of the same alphabet as the strokes.
KITWARP will never mint a term for a rest, but a converter reading kuchi-shōga must know
that `Su` is not an unmapped instrument.

### 4.1 One word, different instruments

| Word | Meaning A | Meaning B | Source |

### 4.2 One syllable, unrelated strokes

| Syllable | Tradition A | Tradition B | Tradition C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ka** | darbuka: rim stroke, **non-dominant hand** | taiko: "a beat played on the edge of the drum (fuchi)" | tabla: flat palm on the **bayan** — a damped *bass* stroke |
| **ta** | tabla: dayan, index finger at the rim | mridangam: a right-hand `Valantalai` stroke | taiko: alternate for `ka` (rim) · janggu: `ta`/`tta`, chaepyeon with the yeolchae |
| **te / ti** | tabla: damped syahi strokes | taiko `Te`: the shimedaiko equivalent of `Do` — a *centre* stroke | — |
| **tin / ten** | tabla: dayan, syahi/maidan border, resonant treble | taiko `Ten`: the shimedaiko equivalent of `Don` — the loud centre beat | — |
| **dha** | tabla: bayan + dayan simultaneously | kendang Reog: "dang thak dhak thung glhang", a head stroke | — |
| **na / num / nam** | tabla `Na`: syahi edge, resonant treble | mridangam `Num`/`Nam`: a valantalai stroke | — |

The lesson for reconciliation: **an onomatopoeic syllable is never a safe key.** Two
traditions using `ka` for opposite ends of the frequency range is the norm, not an edge
case.

### 4.3 Same instrument, sources disagree

- **Tabla `Tin`.** I1: "placing the last two fingers … lightly against the syahi and
  striking on the border between the syahi and the maidan". I2: "hit the maidan with your
  index finger". Different finger, different zone, same name.
- **Tabla `Ta`.** I1 puts it on the rim with pressure on the syahi edge; I2 makes it a
  synonym of `Na` on the chanti. I4 puts `Na` and `Tin` in the same class (RT) but does not
  list `Ta` at all.
- **Conga bass tone.** C2: "somewhat off center on the head". C1 (Pertout): "striking and
  retracting the palm from the **center** of the drum head". A one-value disagreement on
  the `position` axis, from the two most authoritative conga sources reached.
- **Conga stroke count.** C2 lists 11 named tones; C1 lists 8; round-A teaching sources list
  5. C2 itself concedes there is "yet to achieve consensus in naming these sounds".
- **Djembe third stroke.** W1 gives `tonpalo` / "third slap" / `lé` as one sound with three
  names — and it is absent from every French pedagogical source reached (W7 teaches three
  strokes only).

### 4.4 Different words, same physical gesture

- conga `tono deslizado` = bongó `bramido` = English "moose call" = a moistened finger
  rubbed across the head.
- conga `manoteo` = conga `heel-toe` = bongó heel-toe = cajón `grave-agudo` = pandeiro
  polegar-then-ponta = **one hand, two attacks, low then high**. Four traditions, four
  names, one motion.
- djembe `claqué` = djembe `slap` = conga `tono seco` — but see §4.5, they are not the same
  hand shape.
- tonbak `riz` + a stroke name = a roll on that stroke. Rather than a separate `roll` term,
  the tradition compounds; v0.1 factors the same information as `technique` + `ornament`.

### 4.5 Same word, different physical gesture — the dangerous ones

- **`slap`.** conga (C1): "the ridge of the palm, while the extended fingers in an open
  position simultaneously strike and grab the drum head". djembe (W1): "the contact area is
  limited to the edge of the palm and the fingertips". darbuka (M8): a cupped palm with
  relaxed fingers at the edge, "a louder accented version of the tek". Three different hand
  shapes. The tonbak has no slap at all; its bright accent is a **snap** (`pelang`), which
  is a different mechanism entirely.
- **`bass`.** conga and djembe: flat/cupped palm at the centre. darbuka `doum`: "the length
  of the fingers and palm … taking off the hand". tonbak `tom`: **fingertips only**, hand
  cupped. Same name, same pitch region, materially different contact.
- **`rim`.** timbales: the metal hoop, distinct from the `cáscara` (the shell). tabla
  `kinar`/`chanti`: a band of the *skin*, not a hoop. taiko `fuchi`: "the edge of the top
  and bottom of the **drum**", i.e. the body edge. Three different physical objects.
- **`shell`.** timbales `cáscara` is a primary playing surface with its own repertoire; on a
  kit the shell is a novelty. v0.1 `site.shell` (id 5) exists but was minted for the kit
  sense.

### 4.6 Collisions with vocabulary v0.1 specifically

- **`heel` (technique 13) and `toe` (technique 14)** are, in v0.1, hi-hat **pedal** terms —
  a foot on a footboard. In every tradition in this bucket, heel and toe are **hand** terms
  (heel of the palm, tips of the fingers). Same slugs, different limb, different instrument
  family. Identifiers are forever (ADR-0003), so the kit sense cannot be renamed; a
  world-percussion mint will need distinct slugs (e.g. `palm-heel`, `finger-tip`) with a
  documented note that the collision is deliberate. **This is the single most likely
  reconciliation error in the whole bucket.**
- **`thumb` (technique 15)** in v0.1 is a hi-hat/kit term. In pandeiro it is *the* primary
  bass stroke, with open and damped variants (B3 #1, #2), and it is also a **contact**, not
  a technique — B3 pairs it with a damping state and a position to make four distinct
  sounds. If `thumb` stays on `technique`, the pandeiro's four thumb strokes cannot be
  factored.
- **`tambourine` (instrument 33)** is one v0.1 term. The frame-drum family it stands in for
  has, by M2 alone, 25 distinct named instruments, plus the pandeiro's separately-playable
  **jingles** as a site.
- **`contact` = tip | shank | butt** is a stick vocabulary. Every hand tradition in this
  bucket needs palm, palm-ridge/heel, flat-hand, cupped-hand, finger-pads, fingertips,
  four-fingers, individual fingers (index / middle / ring / little), thumb, fingernail,
  wrist, knuckles, and — for the cajón — the **heel of the foot** ("con el talón del pie
  sobre los laterales", X1).
- **`implement` has `hand` and `finger` but no `fist`, no `fingernail`, no `palm`.** The
  brief's prose lists fist and fingernail; `vocabulary/axes.json` v0.1.0 (serial 1) does
  not contain them. `tono de uñas` and B3 #9/#12 need `fingernail` at minimum.

## 5. Gaps against vocabulary v0.1

v0.1.0 (serial 1) has 155 terms, drum kit only; percussion, orchestral, electronic and
utility families are reserved and unminted. So "everything in §2 is missing" is true but
useless. The useful gaps are at the axis level, ordered by how much they cost if left
unfixed.

### 5.1 Axis-value gaps that block minting

| Axis | Missing values | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| `contact` | palm, palm-heel/ridge, flat-hand, cupped-hand, finger-pads, fingertips, four-fingers, index, middle, ring, little, thumb, fingernail, wrist, knuckles, foot-heel | §2.1.3, §2.4 (I8), §2.5, §2.10, §2.11; the axis currently holds three stick terms |
| `site` | jingles/platinelas, vessel-mouth, vessel-neck, vessel-body, head-band (tabla kinar), head-ring (maidan), head-centre-patch (syahi), body-edge (fuchi), wood-hide-join (janggu deok), box-side (cajón lateral) | §2.3, §2.4, §2.8, §2.10, §2.11 |
| `technique` | snap, pitch-bend/press, friction-rub, flip/re-orient | §3.4 (2), (3), (4), (8) |
| `damping` | a value distinguishing cross-hand damping from striking-hand damping | §3.4 (9) |
| `implement` | fingernail, fist, palm (if hand-parts stay on `implement` rather than `contact`), tamarind stick (galan), bachi, gungchae, yeolchae, nylon-thread beater, rosined-friction-stick | §2.7, §2.9, §2.10 |
| `ornament` | a functional-flourish value with indeterminate attack count (`eshareh`/`zinat`) — `attacks: 0` already exists for sustained/indeterminate, so this may be expressible, but the semantics are "grace before an accent", not "sustained" | §2.5 |
| `mechanism` | jingles-on/off, gut-snare-on (bendir), friction-stick | §2.6, §2.10 |

### 5.2 Candidate missing axes

1. **role** — lead / marking / response / on-beat / off-beat, as a property of an ensemble
   member. Evidence: §3.4 (5), five traditions. Currently `instance` on the layout slot
   carries pitch order only, which is a different fact.
2. **pitch-bend or pressure modulation** — an ordered scalar like `openness`, applied to a
   membrane by pressure. Evidence: §3.4 (2), five traditions. It behaves exactly like
   `openness` does for the hi-hat: a continuous physical control with named anchors, already
   measured by a controller in the model's design.
3. **pose / orientation** — §3.4 (4) and (6). Lower priority: it affects timbre but is
   rarely notated as an articulation, `virado` being the exception.

### 5.3 What v0.1 already gets right, confirmed by this bucket

`technique` already contains `slap` (9), `open-tone` (10), `bass-tone` (11), `mute-stroke`
(12), `heel` (13), `toe` (14), `thumb` (15), `gliss` (21), `scrape` (19), `shake` (20). That
is, precisely, the recurring cross-family set of §3.1, minted from kit and Latin-percussion
evidence before this bucket looked at Guinea, Iran, Japan, Korea or Java. **The bucket found
no tradition that needs a primary stroke term outside that set, except `snap`.** The
`ornament.attacks` field is likewise vindicated: taiko's `DoKo`/`DoRo`/`TsuKu`/`KaRa` are
literally defined as "2 fast beats", and the tonbak's roll names are compounds of
`riz` + a base stroke.

### 5.4 Naming risk to flag for the identifier registry

The `heel`/`toe`/`thumb` collision (§4.6) is the one that will silently corrupt data:
a converter that maps a conga `toque de talón` onto `technique.heel` will produce a term
whose kit reading is "hi-hat pedal, heel down". Because identifiers are forever, the
resolution has to be new slugs plus a documented `correction`-style alias note, not a
rename.

### 6.2 The single most authoritative source NOT obtained

**B. Michael Williams, *Selected works for solo frame drums* (DMA dissertation, University of
North Texas), `https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc11019/`.** It is open
access, it is a doctoral dissertation rather than a teaching site, and it covers the exact
family — riq, tar, bendir, daf, pandeiro, tambourine — where this bucket's evidence is
thinnest and most dependent on authority-C sources. Everything §2.6 says about Arabic and
Turkish frame-drum strokes rests on encyclopaedia entries and one trade site; Williams would
replace all of it. **Recommended for the reconciliation pass to chase**, with a non-WebFetch
client.

### 6.3 Known weaknesses in what was extracted

- **Wikipedia carries too much weight.** 12 of the 30 reached sources are Wikipedia
  articles. They are cited and internally consistent, and for `Conga`, `Djembe` and
  `Goblet drum` they carry material (the Spanish stroke list, the frequency measurements,
  the `pa`/`sak` definition) that no reached authority-A source duplicated — but a second
  independent confirmation is missing for most of §2.1.2, §2.2 and §2.7.
- **Two traditions rest on one source each.** Beijing opera on E7, Balinese ensemble roles
  on E10. Both are authority A and both are internally complete, but neither is
  cross-checked.
- **The Brazilian material is strong for pandeiro (B3, a dissertation with a verbatim
  appendix) and weak for everything else.** Surdo, tamborim, cuíca, agogô, caixa, ganzá,
  reco-reco and zabumba rest on Wikipedia alone; the surdo open/`abafado` pair — one of the
  most-used articulations in the whole bucket — is explicitly marked UNVERIFIED in §2.10.
- **The WebSearch budget (200 calls) is one session-wide pool shared by all twelve round-2
  workers, and it was exhausted immediately after this bucket's round A.** Round B therefore
  proceeded by direct URL only, which is why sabar, Javanese kendang and Peruvian cajón were
  not closed: reaching them needed a search, not a fetch. Any future worker on this bucket
  should spend its share of the pool on those three targets first.
- **`web.archive.org` is unreachable in this environment** (connections reset mid-tunnel,
  confirmed by the supervisor for both curl and WebFetch), so the brief's Wayback fallback
  was never available. `archive.org` itself answers and its metadata and full-text search
  work; the *Encyclopedia of Percussion* (X5) is nonetheless behind controlled lending and
  was not opened. Several other hosts answered 403 or 503 to CONNECT and are recorded as
  unreachable in §1 rather than retried.
- **No stroke was verified by listening.** Every physical description is as the source
  words it. Where two sources word the same stroke differently (§4.3) this dossier records
  the disagreement rather than resolving it, per the house rule that a documented gap beats
  a guessed note.

### 6.5 Answer to the question referred here by the vendor-glossary bucket

`cáscara`, `paila` and `abanico` are **closed in §2.2.1**, with text definitions and
locators, and the vendor-glossary dossier's UNVERIFIED entry can cite them:

- `cáscara` — Marc Dicciani, *Intro to Afro-Cuban Mambo Rhythms and Drumset Patterns*,
  © 2009, p. 1, `http://www.dicciani.com/materials/afro_cuban_intro_and_basics.pdf`.
  Authority A/B, a named university music director's own teaching text. Verbatim quotation
  in §2.2.1, including the sentence that decides the modelling question: "cascara refers
  more to the rhythm itself than to the drum on which it is played."
- `paila` — two incompatible senses, both cited (§2.2.1, §4.1): the instrument (Wikipedia
  ES, verbatim Spanish) and the shell (Rhythm Notes). A note named `paila` is ambiguous on
  its face.
- `abanico` — Rhythm Notes, `https://rhythmnotes.net/how-to-play-timbales/`. Authority C,
  but a specific physical definition: rimshot on the macho, 7- or 9-stroke roll on beat
  four, resolving with a rimshot on beat one, used as a section-change cue. **The round-A
  characterisation of the abanico as a "flam-like sweep from rim to head" is contradicted
  by this and should be dropped, not carried as unverified.** A primary-source confirmation
  (Rebeca Mauleón, *Salsa Guidebook for Piano and Ensemble*, Sher Music 1993, is the standard
  reference and has a glossary) was not obtained and would upgrade this from C to A.
- `baqueteo` — bonus, Wikipedia *Danzón*: an open/muted alternation, therefore a `damping`
  fact rather than a stroke name.
